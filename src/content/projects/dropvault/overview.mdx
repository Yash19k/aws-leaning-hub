DropVault is a file-sharing app. You sign in, drop a file in, and get a link you can paste, a QR code someone can scan, or an email sent straight to their inbox. You decide how long the link works (15 minutes up to 7 days, or never) and, if you like, how many downloads it allows. You can turn a link off at any time, and see who downloaded what.

## A story you have probably lived

It's 11 pm before a lab exam. Your classmate Ravi missed the last session and asks for your notes. The PDF is 40 MB, and you don't really want it sitting in a group chat forever.

So you open DropVault, upload `lab-notes.pdf`, and pick **1 day** and **3 downloads**. Ravi gets an email with a Download button. He grabs the notes, his roommate grabs a copy, and after the exam the link quietly stops working. If someone forwards it next semester, they get a polite "Link expired" page instead of your notes.

## Who would use it

- **Students** sharing notes, recordings or project builds with a group, without making them public.
- **Teachers and club leads** handing out one file to a room full of people: put the QR code on the projector.
- **Anyone** sending something private (a signed form, an ID scan) who wants the link to die after one download.

## What makes it interesting under the hood

It is the beginner project on this site, but it is real software, and three ideas make it worth studying.

**1. File bytes never pass through Lambda.** When you upload, a Lambda function only checks who you are and hands back a <Term t="presigned URL">A normal S3 address with a signature and an expiry time in it. Whoever holds it can do one thing (upload or download one file) until it expires, without any AWS keys.</Term>. Your browser then sends the file directly to S3. Files over 100 MB go up in parts, four at a time, and a dropped connection only costs the part in flight. Think of a hotel: reception (Lambda) gives you a key card for one room, but you carry your own luggage up in the lift.

**2. Links are short codes, not S3 URLs.** A presigned S3 URL is about 1,500 characters long, too dense for a QR code to scan, and it stops working when the Lambda's temporary credentials expire, after a few hours at most. So DropVault saves a random 12-character code in DynamoDB and shares `.../v1/d/Xk2p9vQa7LmR` instead. Each time someone opens it, a Lambda checks the code and redirects them to a fresh S3 URL that lives only 5 minutes.

**3. Two layers of expiry.** Every shared link has its own rules (expiry time, download limit, turned off or not), checked on every visit. Underneath, the S3 URL behind each visit expires after 5 minutes. Even if someone copies the final download address, it is useless almost straight away.

<Callout type="info" title="And a little housekeeping magic">
If you close the tab halfway through a big upload and never come back, DynamoDB's <Term t="TTL">Time to live: a timestamp on an item. After that time passes, DynamoDB deletes the item by itself.</Term> deletes the unfinished record after a day, and that deletion wakes a cleanup Lambda which throws away the parts already in S3. Nobody has to run a cleanup script.
</Callout>
